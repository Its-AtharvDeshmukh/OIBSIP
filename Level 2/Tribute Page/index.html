1856 — 1943

Nikola Tesla

He wired the modern world for alternating current, then spent his last savings imagining the ones after it.

Nikola Tesla designed the alternating-current system that still carries electricity into most of the world's homes, then spent the second half of his life chasing ideas — wireless power, remote control, radio — that were often decades ahead of the century he was living in.

Early life

Born at midnight, during a storm

Tesla was born on 10 July 1856 in Smiljan, a village in the Military Frontier of the Austrian Empire, in what is now Croatia. His father, Milutin, was a Serbian Orthodox priest; his mother, Đuka, never went to school but was known for her sharp memory and for building her own household tools and machines. Tesla later credited her for his ability to picture inventions in complete detail before ever building them.

He studied engineering at the Austrian Polytechnic in Graz and later took courses at the University of Prague, though he left without finishing a degree. In 1881 he moved to Budapest to work for a telephone company, and it was there, walking through a park with a friend, that he later said the idea for a rotating magnetic field — the principle behind AC motors — came to him whole, sketched out in the sand with a stick.

Edison, Westinghouse, and the currents

A rivalry that decided how cities would be wired

Tesla emigrated to the United States in 1884 and briefly worked for Thomas Edison's company in New York, improving Edison's direct-current generators. The two disagreed sharply about the future of electrical power — Edison backed direct current (DC), while Tesla was convinced that alternating current (AC) could travel farther and cheaper. Tesla left within the year and eventually sold his AC patents to George Westinghouse, whose company used them to challenge Edison's DC systems directly.

The rivalry, later nicknamed the "War of the Currents," was settled in Tesla and Westinghouse's favor: their AC system lit the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, and two years later it was chosen to harness Niagara Falls, sending electricity to Buffalo, New York, over long-distance power lines for the first time.

Wireless dreams

Chasing power without wires

With his AC royalties, Tesla built laboratories in New York and, later, a research station in Colorado Springs, where he experimented with high-voltage, high-frequency electricity — producing artificial lightning and researching how power and signals might be sent through the air. He returned to New York to build Wardenclyffe Tower on Long Island, intended as a station for worldwide wireless communication and power transmission. Funding collapsed before the project could be finished, and the tower was demolished in 1917.

Later years

Ideas that outran his finances

Tesla continued to patent and propose new devices into the 1920s and 1930s — including early concepts related to radar and remote-controlled machines — while living in a series of New York hotels, often leaving unpaid bills behind him. He never married, said he preferred to devote himself entirely to his work, and became known in his final years for his fondness for feeding pigeons in city parks. He died alone in a Manhattan hotel room on 7 January 1943, at the age of 86.

Major achievements

  1. 01

    The rotating magnetic field

    Patented the principle behind the modern AC induction motor, still the basis of most alternating-current machinery today.

  2. 02

    Won the "War of the Currents"

    His AC system, licensed to George Westinghouse, prevailed over Edison's direct-current standard for long-distance power delivery.

  3. 03

    Harnessed Niagara Falls

    Tesla and Westinghouse's system carried the first long-distance AC power from Niagara Falls to Buffalo in 1896.

  4. 04

    The Tesla coil

    Invented the high-frequency resonant transformer that still bears his name, foundational to early radio technology.

  5. 05

    Named the SI unit of magnetic flux density

    In 1960, the General Conference on Weights and Measures named the "tesla" in his honor.

Why it still matters

The alternating-current system Tesla designed is still, more than a century later, the way electricity moves through power grids worldwide — it's why homes can be wired to substations miles away rather than needing a generator on every block. The Tesla coil he invented for his high-frequency experiments became a foundational tool in early radio technology, and his patents on remote-controlled devices anticipated technology that took decades to become practical.

His life is also a reminder that being right early doesn't guarantee reward: Tesla died with little money, his Wardenclyffe project unfinished, and much of his later work overlooked until long after his death. The 1960 decision to name the SI unit of magnetic flux density after him — the tesla — was, in a sense, physics catching up with an engineer it had spent decades taking for granted.
